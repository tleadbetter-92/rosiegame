# World Lore Update: Civilisation, the Second Collapse, and the New Revelation

**Status:** WORKING SYNTHESIS OF RECENT DISCUSSION  
**Purpose:** Capture the lore developed since the earlier foundational files. This is a separate update file, not a replacement for existing lore files. Established thematic direction is recorded alongside ideas that remain provisional.

## 1. The Old Kingdom and the New Empire

“Old Kingdom” and “New Empire” are working labels, not necessarily official names. The Old Kingdom refers to an older, sophisticated civilisational tradition: cities, roads, law, engineering, professional armies and established institutions. It declined and fragmented over a long period, but need not have vanished completely.

The New Empire is a more recent political power that arose later and became a dominant centralised civilisation in its own territories. It may have inherited parts of the older civilisation rather than replacing it outright.

Their precise political relationship is intentionally vague. They are separate political centres within a connected civilisational world, with shared traditions and overlapping history. At different times they may have been allies, rivals, nominally subordinate to one another, or bound by treaties. The New Empire may claim to be civilisation's rightful continuation; Old Kingdom rulers or scholars may dispute that claim.

The Eastern and Western Roman Empires are an analogy for the feel of this relationship, not a blueprint. Do not assume precise borders or a legal arrangement unless decided later.

## 2. The War of the Two Crowns and the First Collapse

The old civilisational world once lived alongside magic. Rulers and scholars used it to overcome suffering, disease, hunger, ageing and death. These uses brought real benefits, but the pursuit of power increasingly became coercive and extractive.

The War of the Two Crowns devastated the old order. The victorious side blamed magic itself for the catastrophe, banned it and persecuted practitioners, suppressing or destroying knowledge. The New Empire's anti-magic order emerged from the aftermath, though the precise relationship between the war, the Old Kingdom's fragmentation and the Empire's rise remains open.

The war did not necessarily create the Old/New distinction by itself. The Old Kingdom was already an older civilisation, and the New Empire is “newer” in a broad historical sense. The war accelerated or reshaped an existing transition.

## 3. The Two Great Powers and Their Shared Error

The New Empire and the Everlasting Court are different expressions of the same deeper problem: civilisation's attempt to dominate life through an abstract ideal.

- **The New Empire** represents excessive order, centralisation, uniformity and control. It brings real benefits such as law, security, roads, trade and administration, but increasingly treats people as components of the state.
- **The Everlasting Court** represents the pursuit of power without limits: overcoming natural constraints, death and human weakness through magic. Its pursuit of liberation and perfection becomes exploitative, treating living beings as resources.

They may fight one another, but they are not moral opposites at the deepest level. Both separate human power from a healthy relationship with the living world. Their conflict is real, but it is not the whole story.

## 4. Magic as an Allegory

Magic is the world's concrete analogue for the human spirit and personality: imagination, desire, ambition, creativity, cruelty and the capacity to transform the world.

The player's world readily understands that magic is not inherently good or evil; its meaning depends on how it is used and the relationship people have with it. In this fictional world, most civilised people instead categorise magic through rigid divisions: good or evil, permitted or forbidden, ours or theirs. Their in-group/out-group thinking makes the new idea revolutionary.

The underlying issue is not simply whether a particular magical act is good or bad. It is the relationship between human power, the spirit and the living world.

## 5. The Tribal Peoples

The tribal peoples live closer to nature, ancestry and inherited customs. They do not necessarily ask whether magic is inherently good or evil. Magic is part of the world they inhabit, like human nature itself. Their knowledge is embedded in practice, kinship, stories, taboos, experience and tradition, rather than abstract written theories.

They are an analogy for an older, Eden-like relationship with nature, before advanced civilisation's abstractions and ambitions separated human life from its wider ecological context. They are not simply a third political faction that consciously synthesises the Empire and the Court, and should not deliver philosophical lectures about balance.

They preserve something valuable, but are fragmented, vulnerable and unable to withstand the expanding civilisations indefinitely. Their decline or absorption may be inevitable on the broad historical scale, though the player can still preserve particular communities, people or knowledge. They are not destined to save the world.

## 6. The New Revelation: Jesus and the Secret Society

A figure modelled on Jesus has just appeared in the world's present. His message challenges the dominant assumptions about magic and the human spirit. He knowingly sacrifices himself; the sacrifice is central to his teaching, while the exact historical circumstances and precise meaning remain to be developed.

His revelation offers a conscious moral and spiritual framework for guiding human power. It rejects both the Empire's attempt to suppress magic as such and the Court's attempt to pursue power without limits. The teaching is not a compromise between existing political factions; it introduces a new way of understanding the spirit and its relationship with life.

His followers form a small, persecuted movement and are connected to, or become the core of, the secret magical society previously discussed. They are the primary point of moral and emotional recognition for the intended player experience. Individual followers can still be flawed, uncertain, divided or inconsistent.

The tribal peoples and the Christian movement are not the same. The tribal peoples preserve an older, largely untheorised relationship with nature; the new movement offers a conscious moral revelation in a world where civilisation's power has made its dangers acute.

The movement is not necessarily the player's required faction. The player may never discover it, may reject it, or may support another side.

## 7. The Second Collapse: Three Developments

The world is approaching a second historical breakdown. Three developments are unfolding, and they may be causally connected without being fully understood by the people living through them.

1. **The dark forces are growing stronger.** Attacks and strange events increase, and powers associated with the Everlasting Court become more active.
2. **The New Empire is becoming unstable.** Discontent grows alongside its civilising achievements. Some people resent centralised control, restrictions on magic, taxation or the displacement of local customs. Different people oppose the Empire for different reasons; not all discontent belongs to the Christian movement.
3. **A revolution begins in the distant Old Kingdom.** A Christian-inspired movement challenges the established order and could eventually provoke another civil war. This revolution is not necessarily a conflict between the Old Kingdom and the New Empire.

One possible connection is that civilisational instability drives some people toward forbidden magic, old loyalties and alternative sources of power. At the same time, political fragmentation weakens the ability of the kingdoms to contain existing threats. The Everlasting Court may deliberately exploit the crisis without having caused all of it.

The Christian revolution can arise from the same underlying social crisis without being responsible for the dark forces. It may threaten the current political balance while also offering a different long-term direction.

The second collapse repeats the underlying historical problem in a new form: the civilisation that arose to prevent the first catastrophe has not solved the deeper problem.

## 8. The Frontier and the Strategic Choke Point

A strategic choke point helps hold the dark forces back from the wider civilised world. The existing military and political balance prevents the Everlasting Court or its associated forces from expanding freely.

The rise of the dark forces and the fracture of the kingdoms could destabilise this frontier. If the Empire becomes consumed by civil war, it may lose the capacity to defend it. This creates a dilemma: suppressing political change might preserve the frontier while perpetuating an oppressive order; revolution might create the possibility of renewal while also opening immediate dangers.

The player may eventually influence the choke point. Possible outcomes include preserving the stalemate, weakening its defences and allowing hostile forces through, or entering enemy territory and attempting to destroy the threat at its source. These are possible directions, not a locked quest structure.

## 9. The Forest Attacks and the Hidden People

An old tribal people, believed by most to have been destroyed after the War of the Two Crowns, has secretly survived for at least a thousand years. They remained loyal to the defeated side and live in a hard-to-find refuge deep in the forest, protected by concealed entrances, caves and defensible passages.

Their home is difficult to conquer but cannot support an unlimited population or a large conventional army. Their small numbers and difficult terrain explain how they can survive without becoming a major military power.

Attacks around the forest and lumber settlement have become more frequent. Hostile creatures attack targets while one or several distinct wizardly figures may accompany them, appear to direct or herd them, and then withdraw. The existing creature-attack mechanic can remain, but the creatures' identity, origin and exact control method are deliberately undecided.

The hidden people may now be receiving secret support from the Everlasting Court. The Court may be exploiting their inherited loyalty and grievances rather than having created the tribe. The precise relationship remains provisional.

Most local people regard stories of the hidden people as myth because the accepted history says they were wiped out. Godwin, the innkeeper, can repeat the rumour without believing it. The player may witness the unusual figures or discover evidence gradually; the game should not reveal the hidden refuge before credible clues lead there.

## 10. The Intended Narrative Reveal

At first, the player may see a conflict between oppressive civilisations and tribal peoples who appear to represent a freer, more natural life. It is reasonable for the player to assume the tribal peoples are the good side and might eventually prevail.

As play continues, that interpretation changes:
- The tribal peoples preserve something valuable but cannot simply replace civilisation or save the world.
- The Empire and the Everlasting Court appear opposed but share a deeper error.
- The frontier attacks are part of a larger, still partly hidden historical crisis.
- The Empire is fracturing, a revolution is emerging in the Old Kingdom, and dark forces are growing.
- The Christian-inspired movement introduces another possibility, but the player is not forced to discover or support it.

The beginning should not be revealed as entirely false. The Empire really can be oppressive, the Court genuinely dangerous, and the tribal peoples genuinely valuable. What changes is the player's understanding of what these facts mean and whether defeating one apparent enemy can solve the underlying problem.

## 11. World Simulation and Player Freedom

These large developments should exist in the world independently of the player's knowledge. NPCs and factions should know only what they plausibly know; rumours can be incomplete or mistaken. The player can focus on local life, take sides in conflicts, explore the hidden history or intervene in major events.

The world should not force the player to follow the Christian movement or any particular faction. The intended moral structure can remain clear to the author while the player retains agency over discovery and action. The simulation should record actual state changes and consequences rather than allowing narration alone to determine outcomes.

## Open Questions — Do Not Treat as Settled

- Official names of the Old Kingdom, New Empire, Everlasting Court and hidden people.
- The precise political and territorial relationship between the Old Kingdom and New Empire.
- The exact sequence linking the War of the Two Crowns, the Old Kingdom's fragmentation and the Empire's rise.
- The Christ-like figure's name, biography, teachings and circumstances of sacrifice.
- The exact relationship between his followers and the secret magical society.
- The precise nature, cause and timing of the revolution in the Old Kingdom.
- Whether the Everlasting Court deliberately engineers the second collapse or mainly exploits it.
- The identity, origin and control mechanism of the creatures in the forest attacks.
- The hidden people's present goals and the extent of their alliance with the Court.
- The exact nature and location of the strategic choke point, and the possible endgame outcomes.

This file captures the recent discussion as a working synthesis. It should be kept separate for now and reconciled with the earlier foundational allegory and forest-attack files only when the user chooses to consolidate the lore.
